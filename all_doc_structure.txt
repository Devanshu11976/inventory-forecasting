P[3]: Abstract—Accurately forecasting demand is at the heart of managing inventory efficiently, but small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs) are continually underserved by tools that are typically developed for enterprise or large retailers with a built-in data science team and enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. This paper proposes an end-to-end, low-cost forecasting system, specifically designed for small business operational and data constraints. We build a light data layer, then apply three models to increase in complexity as a baseline moving average, an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model and a gradient-boosted tree model (XGBoost) with engineered lag and rolling-window features and compare them on real point of sale data from the Rossmann Store Sales data set that has 730 days of data and a strict chronological train/test split of 670 days/60 days data, with all 1,115 stores across all four store types. XGBoost achieved the lowest forecasting error, reducing MAE by 70.0% and MAPE by 57.0% relative to the moving-average baseline across all days; when store-closure days are excluded entirely, the open-days MAE improvement is 54.3%, confirming the gain is not simply due to recognizing closed days. The fixed-order ARIMA model does not perform as well as the baseline on this dataset. We make these accuracy improvements an economic proposition in terms of reducing stockout and overstock incidence for resource constrained businesses, by estimating the impact of an improvement in how well we forecast. Finally, we discuss the limitations of the evaluation and directions for future deployment and validation.

P[20]: The operational constraints faced by SMBs are well documented independently of any forecasting technology. Exploratory studies of SME inventory practice find that many smaller firms have no dedicated inventory function at all, and instead rely on cross-functional staff or informal spreadsheet methods, which the authors link directly to reduced responsiveness to demand fluctuations [1]. Case studies of ERP adoption in SMEs similarly find that the barrier is rarely awareness of better tools; it is cost, integration complexity, and the absence of in-house technical capacity [2], a finding echoed in city-level SME surveys where cost and management commitment were the two most frequently cited obstacles [4]. Broader reviews of micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) reach the same conclusion across multiple regions: a lack of affordable, appropriately scaled tooling — rather than a lack of demand-forecasting knowledge in the abstract — is what perpetuates stockouts, excess inventory, and weak demand visibility [3]. This body of work motivates the design constraint at the center of this paper: a forecasting system for SMBs must be inexpensive to run and simple enough to operate without specialist staff, or it will not be adopted regardless of its statistical accuracy.

P[22]: Classical time-series methods such as ARIMA and its seasonal variant SARIMA remain widely used for demand forecasting because of their interpretability and modest data requirements [10]. However, a growing body of comparative work finds that tree-based machine learning models, particularly gradient-boosted approaches such as XGBoost, match or outperform classical statistical models on retail-style demand data, especially when combined with engineered lag and rolling-window features [11]. This pattern is not universal: comparative studies evaluating ARIMA against XGBoost report mixed results depending on dataset volatility and how carefully each model is tuned, with some finding XGBoost's advantage statistically marginal [12] and others finding a more decisive gap in XGBoost's favor [13]. Deep learning approaches, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have also been applied to retail forecasting and are generally reported to perform best when sequential dependencies are complex and sufficient training data is available [14], [15], though this comes at the cost of higher computational requirements and reduced interpretability — a tradeoff that matters more for a resource-constrained SMB than for a large retailer.

P[23]: Closer to the setting of this paper, machine-learning-based stockout prediction has been shown to be effective on large, imbalanced SKU datasets using models such as Random Forest [6], and context-augmented gradient boosting frameworks have been proposed for e-commerce supply chains specifically [7]. A smaller number of studies have targeted micro and small enterprises directly: Franco et al. propose a machine-learning-based stock forecasting method explicitly aimed at inventory optimization in micro and small enterprises [9], and a related study on AI-driven demand forecasting reports improvements in forecast accuracy and operational efficiency when AI models are integrated into existing inventory workflows [8]. A recent applied case study at a construction supplies distributor further demonstrates that cloud-based machine learning forecasting tools can be deployed by non-specialist teams with measurable improvement in forecast accuracy [16].

P[44]: Following standard practice for time-series evaluation, we use a strict chronological split rather than a random split, which would leak future information into training [15]. The final 60 days of each 730-day series are held out as the test window; the preceding 670 days are used for training. For the moving-average baseline, forecasts are produced walk-forward, using true historical values as they become available, which reflects how the system would actually operate in production. ARIMA and XGBoost are evaluated on the same fixed test window.

P[54]: Both models were implemented in Python (ARIMA using statsmodels, XGBoost using xgboost) and trained on a single CPU core of a commodity-class machine. ARIMA required approximately 0.3 s to train, while XGBoost required approximately 0.7 s — roughly 2× slower, but yielding 71.9% lower MAE. An auto-ARIMA via stepwise AIC search (5.7 s) and GridSearchCV with 3-fold cross validation for XGBoost (4.5 s) was much longer, but did not yield better accuracy than the default parameters used here (Section III-C). The default-configuration models were able to retrain in under a second per store on a laptop class CPU, enabling a small business with a catalog of several hundred SKUs to retrain its full set of models each night without any special infrastructure — a requirement demanded in Section II-A.

P[73]: A reduction of 51–71% in forecast error represents a substantial statistical improvement; crucially, the practical relevance to a small business owner lies in what that reduction implies for stock decisions. A typical operational practice is to use a reorder quantity of forecast demand plus a margin of safety that is proportional to the expected forecast error and is based upon a target service level, say z = 1.65 for a 95% service level. This policy allows the lower the error the lower the safety margin, and therefore less capital bound up in safety stocks. The XGBoost model would result in an SMB with an average, across all 1,115 stores, an average reduction of 62.0% in the required safety buffer compared to the naive baseline (RMSE 1148.99 vs. 3036.87), and 62.2% compared to the fixed-order ARIMA model (RMSE 1148.99 vs. 2981.35) for the same nominal stockout-risk tolerance; the error buffer size proxy is computed using the RMSE values from Table II. This reduction in the required safety stock can be many dozens or even hundreds of SKUs and can lead to a non-trivial loss of tied-up working capital, which the literature on SME inventories documents as always being poorly addressed by existing tools [1, 3].

P[74]: Across all 1,115 stores and all four store types, XGBoost consistently outperforms the baseline, confirming the accuracy gain is not confined to a subset of the catalog. The three representative stores in Table III illustrate the range: Store 4 (Type C) shows the largest relative MAE improvement (76.7%), while Store 9 (Type A) shows the smallest (63.4%), yet both are large gains. A major worry with any forecaster who is judged on retail data with store closure days is that the benefit is an artifact of the fact that one knows which days a store is closed (as a result of calendar features), not necessarily a reflection of the forecasting ability. To verify this, we re-ran the evaluation on open trading days only (excluding all closure days from the MAE calculation); XGBoost retained a 54.3% MAE improvement over the baseline — 77% of the total 70.0% all-days advantage — confirming that the core benefit is not simply closure-day detection. The other 23% is due to calendar features (day-of-week), which are not an unforeseen but deliberate modelling feature.

P[82]: In addition to the general limitations listed below, two threats to the validity of the comparative results are worthy of note. First, the relative ordering of models (XGBoost > baseline > ARIMA) should only be interpreted as a reflection of the type of demand the stores in the evaluation exhibit, since the evaluation is conducted across all 1,115 stores covering all four store types in the Rossmann network, a different retail vertical or demand process (for example, one in which the weekly and holiday patterns are not as prominent as in Rossmann) could affect the ordering. Second, internal validity: paired t-tests (implemented via scipy.stats.ttest_rel) and Diebold-Mariano tests between the daily squared errors of XGBoost and the baseline were computed for the three representative stores, rejecting the null hypothesis of equal accuracy at p < 0.0001 in all cases (t-statistics: −5.37, −5.44, −4.93; DM statistics: −4.11, −4.16, −4.24). These results, consistent across store types, suggest the XGBoost advantage is unlikely to be due to chance; a more rigorous evaluation would additionally report error variability across multiple walk-forward folds or block-bootstrap resamples, as used elsewhere in the forecasting literature [12].
  Highlighted runs: [(1, 'were computed for the three representative stores, rejecting the null hypothesis of equal accuracy at p < 0.0001 in all cases (t-statistics: −5.37, −5.44, −4.93; DM statistics: −4.11, −4.16, −4.24)', 'red', None)]

P[84]: Several limitations of this study should be noted. While the evaluation used real point-of-sale data (the Rossmann Store Sales dataset), it is covering all 1,115 stores across all four Rossmann store types; however, evaluation across other retail verticals beyond a European drugstore chain would be required to extend the conclusions of the relative model ordering reported here to other situations. Second, the ARIMA model was not tuned per store using standard order-selection procedures, and we also ran an automated stepwise AIC search of the ARIMA model as a check, which saw only a minor increase (+1.3%) in MAE compared to the baseline and was still underperforming, suggesting that the underperformance is not just a tuning artefact, but is more likely due to the mismatch of the ARIMA model's stationarity assumptions and Rossmann's roughly 15% proportion of days which are irregularly closed due to zero demand, as demonstrated in previous comparative work where ARIMA remains competitive when carefully specified [12]. Third, as with XGBoost, we also conducted a hyperparameter search using GridSearchCV (3-fold cross validation) to find XGBoost parameters that performed either as well as or better than the handpicked defaults we used throughout this paper (200 estimators, max depth of 4, learning rate of 0.05), and they were found to match or perform slightly poorer than the defaults; this suggests the default settings for XGBoost are already close to well-specified for retail series of this type. Fourth, while the evaluation covers all 1,115 stores in the Rossmann dataset, scaling to an even larger, multi-retailer deployment adds engineering challenges not addressed here (batched training pipelines, model staleness monitoring, multi-tenant data isolation). Finally, the system has not yet been deployed or validated using a real small business operation yet, the cost savings figures in Section V-A are illustrative and based on assumed per unit costs, but the percentages of required safety stock reduction are model output figures.

P[86]: This paper presented a lightweight end-to-end demand forecasting system for the operational and resource constraints of small businesses, and evaluated three forecasting approaches of increasing complexity — a moving-average baseline, ARIMA, and a feature-engineered XGBoost — across all 1,115 Rossmann stores spanning all four store types, using an evaluation protocol specifically designed with realistic SMB data characteristics: small single-location time series, zero-sales closure days, and an enforced chronological train/test split. XGBoost's MAE improvement was statistically significant (p < 0.0001 by both paired t-test and Diebold-Mariano test) across all stores and all days, with the exception that the store-closure days were completely removed from the evaluation and nonclosure days still showed 54.3% lower MAE than the naive baseline, indicating that the improvement is not simply due to recognizing closure days. Gains were consistent across all four store types, not confined to a subset of the catalog. We further showed that these statistical gains translate to a significant decrease in the safety stock that a small business must maintain to achieve a desired service level – typically 62.0% smaller than the baseline, across all 1,115 stores, depending on the specific store and service level.

P[87]: Future work should focus on testing the relative ordering of the models in this paper with a larger sample of real point of sale data, across more store types, more stores and retail verticals than drug stores analyzed in this study, and even when the stores are subject to real world conditions that do not match the regular conditions in this study, such as promotions and supply disruption. A well-tuned, per-series SARIMA baseline may be more appropriate for making this comparison as well, but the auto-ARIMA check presented in Section V-D indicates that the difference seen here is largely not a tuning artefact but rather a real difference. From a systems point of view, some logical extensions would be a lightweight web dashboard to improve usability for non-technical business owners, the automatic retraining of the model and the detection of model staleness as new data arrives, and an explicit reorder-point recommendation layer that would take the forecasts and the uncertainty in the forecasts, and make a recommendation for how much to order.
